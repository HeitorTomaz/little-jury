# Potted Plants and Sustained Attention in a Middle-School Classroom: A Pilot Study

## Abstract

This pilot study examined whether placing potted plants in a classroom changes students' sustained
attention during independent reading. Twenty-four students aged 12 and 13 from one class took a
ten-minute attention task on two Fridays, three weeks apart: once in their usual room and once after
six potted plants were placed along the windows. Mean scores rose from 61.2 to 66.8 points
(p = 0.048). We conclude that classroom plants improve students' attention and recommend that schools
add plants to every classroom.

## 1. Introduction

Teachers often report that students lose focus during long stretches of independent work. Studies in
offices have linked indoor plants to lower self-reported stress, and a few school studies have
measured changes in mood. Fewer studies have measured attention directly in a classroom, during
ordinary lessons, with a standard task. This pilot asks one question: do students score higher on a
sustained-attention task after potted plants are added to their classroom?

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Participants

The participants were the 24 students of one seventh-grade class (13 girls, 11 boys) at a public
school in a mid-sized town. All students in the class took part, with written consent from a parent.
No student was excluded.

### 2.2 Procedure

On the first Friday, students completed the attention task in their usual classroom, which had no
plants. Over the following weekend, six potted plants (pothos and snake plants, about 60 cm tall)
were placed along the window ledge. On the third Friday after that, students completed the same task
again in the same room, at the same hour, with the same teacher present.

### 2.3 Measure

The attention task was a ten-minute letter-cancellation sheet: students crossed out every target
letter in rows of random letters. The score was the number of correct cancellations minus errors,
scaled to 100 points. The same sheet layout was used both times, with the letters in a new order.

### 2.4 Analysis

We compared the two sessions with a paired t-test. We did not correct for any other variable.

## 3. Results

The mean score was 61.2 (SD 9.4) in the first session and 66.8 (SD 10.1) in the second session.
The difference of 5.6 points was statistically significant (t(23) = 2.09, p = 0.048). Eighteen of
the 24 students scored higher in the second session, five scored lower and one scored the same.

| Session | Mean | SD | n |
|---|---|---|---|
| Without plants | 61.2 | 9.4 | 24 |
| With plants | 66.8 | 10.1 | 24 |

## 4. Discussion

Students paid more attention after the plants were added. This supports the view that contact with
nature restores the capacity to focus, and it suggests that the effect appears even with a small
number of plants and in a short period. Because the plants are cheap and easy to care for, schools
can expect better attention in every grade by adding plants to all classrooms.

The teacher also observed that the room felt calmer in the weeks with plants, and several students
asked to take care of them.

## 5. Limitations

The study was carried out in one class. The weather on the two Fridays was different: the first was
rainy and the second sunny.

## 6. Conclusion

Classroom plants improve students' attention. Schools should add plants to every classroom.
